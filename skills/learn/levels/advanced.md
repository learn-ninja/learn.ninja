# Advanced

For someone with significant learning in the subject who can already apply its methods. Assume its vocabulary, standard models, and usual explanations. The learner wants to analyse cases and judge between competing accounts.

## Summary and objective

- Summary: the prerequisite in the field's words, then the judgment the lesson sharpens: "You know the Late Bronze Age kingdoms traded, fought, and fell together. Weigh the main explanations against what the evidence can show."
- Objective: an analysis or an evaluation: "Explain why no single cause accounts for the collapse."

## Hook

A claim most advanced learners accept that the evidence complicates, or two accounts that both sound right.

## Concepts

- Problem first: the case or the puzzle, then the mechanism. Don't re-explain the model; name it and go to where it bends.
- Teach the tension: what competes, what the evidence supports, what it rules out.
- One real example, complications left in, beats two clean ones.

## Questions

- Ask the learner to analyse and evaluate: which explanation does this evidence support, where is the flaw in this argument, what does this case rule out, which of two accounts is stronger and why.
- Natural bricks: `compare` of two real arguments; `hotspot` for the weak step in an argument, chart, or code; `scenario` where no option is clean; `choice` with `multiple: true` when several claims hold; `sort` by what the evidence supports.
- Wrong options are defensible but weaker: the popular explanation the evidence undercuts, a true statement that doesn't answer the question, a conclusion that overreaches. Feedback says what the option gets right and where it stops holding.
- Hints are sparse: one or two at most, pointing at the frame ("what would this evidence look like under the other account?"), never at a step.

## Challenge

Evaluate a claim, a plan, or a case the lesson hasn't discussed. The best option should take a moment's doubt even for someone who followed everything.

## Takeaways

Judgments and their grounds, not rules: "Treat a single-cause story as a claim to test, not a finding."

## You've drifted if…

- A concept brick explains the fundamentals the summary assumed, even as a one-line recap.
- One option is obviously right and the rest obviously wrong, or the answer sits in the visual above.
- A hint gives a step.

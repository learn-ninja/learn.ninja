# Intermediate

For someone who knows the basics and wants to use them. Assume the field's core terms and its standard method: the learner could follow the beginner lesson on this, but doesn't yet know which rule applies when, or where it breaks.

## Summary and objective

- Summary: name what's assumed, then the gain: "You can read a scatter plot. Learn when it's safe to read a correlation as a cause, and when it isn't."
- Objective: apply the idea to cases the lesson doesn't show: "Spot the third variable or the reversed cause behind a correlation."

## Hook

A realistic case where the basic rule, applied naively, gives the wrong answer. The learner's basics make them confident; the first concept shows the catch.

## Concepts

- Skip the definitions; a half-sentence reminder is enough ("a correlation: the two move together"). Spend the words on the method, when it applies, and the trap.
- Use the field's terms without introducing them. Define only a word that's new at this level.
- One example that isn't the textbook one.

## Questions

- Ask the learner to apply: which method fits this case, carry it out on new values, predict what happens here, run it backwards.
- Natural bricks: `choice` on a fresh case; `scenario` with trade-offs; `order` for a real procedure; `sort` of real cases by which rule applies; `blank` for a missing step; `hotspot` for the wrong step in a worked artifact.
- Wrong options are the right idea misapplied: the right method with one step wrong, the rule used outside where it holds, the neighbouring concept. Feedback says what the option gets right and where it goes wrong.
- Hints on the harder half of the questions. Name the step or the condition to check, not the answer.

## Challenge

A new situation that needs both concepts, or the method and its exception, in two steps of reasoning. Nothing in it should be answerable from the concept text alone.

## Takeaways

When to use the method, and the one condition to check first.

## You've drifted if…

- A concept brick defines a term, or shows a form or table, the summary assumes.
- A question can be answered by rereading the concept, or only recalls a list it showed.
- Wrong options are silly instead of being the right idea misapplied.
